# Sources: foundations, evidence and evaluation

All accessed 2026-09-25.

**Access** says what I actually read:
- **Full**: the full text, or the substantive sections of it.
- **Partial**: some pages or sections.
- **Abstract**: the abstract or publisher summary only.
- **Secondary**: only seen quoted or summarised elsewhere. Every Secondary item is marked **[QUOTED ONLY]**.

Tier follows the brief (1 = primary or authoritative, 2 = professional, 4 = community or practitioner).

## Evaluation methods

**Molich, R. "Are Usability Evaluations Reproducible?"** *ACM Interactions* 25(6), Nov–Dec 2018. http://www.dialogdesign.dk/tekster/are_usability_evaluations_reproducible.pdf. Access: Full. Tier 1.
- Claim: usability evaluations are not reproducible across teams; five users are not enough for coverage; expert reviews are comparable to tests; few false alarms.
- Evidence: 10 CUE studies (1998–2018), more than 140 teams. Table 3 gives single-team issue shares: CUE-2 75%, CUE-4 60%, CUE-9 40%.
- Context: complex commercial websites; mostly professional teams.
- Limitations: problem matching is a judgement call. Validity of issues was judged by expert consensus against "generally accepted advice", not against users. The author ran the studies.

**DialogDesign CUE studies index.** https://www.dialogdesign.dk/CUE.html. Access: Full (index page). Tier 1.
- Claim: lists CUE-1 to CUE-10 with products and team counts.
- Limitations: index only; the details come from Molich 2018.

**Hertzum, M., Molich, R. & Jacobsen, N. E. "What You Get Is What You See: Revisiting the Evaluator Effect in Usability Tests."** *Behaviour & Information Technology* 33(2), 143–161, 2014 (preprint). https://mortenhertzum.dk/publ/BIT2014.pdf. Access: Partial (pp. 1–3: abstract, introduction, related work). Tier 1.
- Claim: the evaluator effect persists among experienced professionals analysing identical videos.
- Evidence: 19 professionals. Detection 33% of all problems (moderated) and 32% (unmoderated); 50% and 40% of severe problems. 24% and 30% of multiply-reported problems were rated critical by one participant and minor by another. Restates Jacobsen et al. 1998 (detection 52%, any-two agreement 42%).
- Limitations: five-user sessions of one website (CUE-9).

**Hertzum, M. & Jacobsen, N. E. "The Evaluator Effect: A Chilling Fact About Usability Evaluation Methods."** *IJHCI* 15(1), 183–204, 2003. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1207/S15327590IJHC1501_14 (ResearchGate copy returned 403). Access: Abstract, plus a detailed restatement in Hertzum et al. 2014. Tier 1.
- Claim: cognitive walkthrough, heuristic evaluation and think-aloud all show a substantial evaluator effect.
- Evidence: review of 11 studies; any-two agreement 5–65%.
- Limitations: I did not read the full text.

**Hertzum, M. & Jacobsen, N. E. "The evaluator effect during first-time use of the cognitive walkthrough technique."** 1999. https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221099962. Access: Secondary (search-result summary of the abstract). **[QUOTED ONLY]** Tier 1.
- Claim: 11 first-time CW evaluators; web library system; three tasks; anchoring and stereotyping in descriptions of users.
- Limitations: no numbers used from it.

**Nielsen, J. & Molich, R. "Heuristic evaluation of user interfaces."** *Proc. CHI '90*, 249–256, April 1990. https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/97243.97281. Access: Abstract (via search summary). Tier 1.
- Claim: individual evaluators found 20–51% of problems; aggregates of 3–5 evaluators do well.
- Limitations: the denominator is the problems found in total, not all real problems.

**Nielsen, J. "The Theory Behind Heuristic Evaluations."** NN/g, 1994-11-01. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-to-conduct-a-heuristic-evaluation/theory-heuristic-evaluations/. Access: Full. Tier 1–2.
- Claim: single evaluators find about 35% (average of six projects); diminishing returns from more evaluators; cost-benefit example.
- Limitations: benefits are estimates, and the projects are the author's own.

**Nielsen, J. "How to Rate the Severity of Usability Problems."** NN/g, 1994-11-01. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-to-rate-the-severity-of-usability-problems/. Access: Full. Tier 1–2.
- Claim: severity combines frequency, impact and persistence, with market impact separate; ratings from a single evaluator are unreliable, so use the mean of at least 3.
- Limitations: no reliability statistics given.

**NN/g. "How to Conduct a Heuristic Evaluation."** 2023-06-25. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-to-conduct-a-heuristic-evaluation/. Access: Full. Tier 2.
- Claim: use 3–5 evaluators; heuristics are "guidelines, not laws"; heuristic evaluation cannot replace user research.
- Limitations: no data.

**Cockton, G., Woolrych, A., Hall, L. & Hindmarch, M. "Changing Analysts' Tunes: The Surprising Impact of a New Instrument for Usability Inspection Method Assessment."** People and Computers XVII (Springer), c. 2003–2004. http://s3.amazonaws.com/publicationslist.org/data/lynnehall/ref-16/confpaper1.pdf. Access: Partial (pp. 1–10). Tier 1.
- Claim: heuristics are poor discovery resources; many false positives; an extended report format improves validity.
- Evidence: restates the first study (99 analysts): 61% of heuristic applications inappropriate, 65% of predictions false positives, 80% of the hardest problems missed. Follow-up: 31 students, 10 groups, a transport website, 5 test users.
- Limitations: student analysts; the exact publication year is uncertain from the PDF.

**Cockton, G. & Woolrych, A. "Sale must end: should discount methods be cleared off HCI's shelves?"** *ACM Interactions* 9(5), Sept 2002. https://interactions.acm.org/archive/view/september-2002/sale-must-end1. Access: Secondary (the page returned 403; argument known from search summaries and Cockton et al. c. 2004). **[QUOTED ONLY]** Tier 1.

**Bailey, R. W. "Heuristic evaluations vs. usability testing."** Human Factors International newsletter, January 2001. https://www.humanfactors.com/newsletters/heuristic_evaluations_vs_usability_testing_the_relative_effectiveness.html. Access: Full. Tier 2.
- Claim: about 36% hits, 43% false alarms and 21% misses, pooled from Catani & Biers 1998, Rooden et al. 1999 and Stanton & Stevenage 1998.
- Limitations: practitioner pooling; the underlying studies were not read by me **[QUOTED ONLY for the three studies]**. Often misattributed online to Bailey, Allan & Raiello 1992.

**Usability Body of Knowledge. "Heuristic Evaluation."** UXPA. https://www.usabilitybok.org/heuristic-evaluation/. Access: Full. Tier 2.
- Claim: summarises Jeffries 1991/1994 (false alarms; heuristic evaluation finds more minor problems), Nielsen 1992 (experts find more), and CUE-4 (no false alarms; the same proportion of major and minor problems).
- Limitations: secondary summary. The underlying Jeffries papers are **[QUOTED ONLY]**.

**Sauro, J. "The Value of Multiple Evaluators in Heuristic Evaluations."** MeasuringU. https://measuringu.com/he-multiple/. Access: Full. Tier 2.
- Claim: with 4 evaluators on two rental sites, 22 issues; 18% found by all; 57% average pairwise agreement.
- Limitations: tiny sample; one practitioner study.

**Gray, W. D. & Salzman, M. C. "Damaged Merchandise? A Review of Experiments that Compare Usability Evaluation Methods."** *HCI* 13(3), 203–261, 1998. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ582171. Access: Abstract. Tier 1.
- Claim: design flaws in five influential method-comparison experiments undermine their conclusions.

**Nielsen, J. "Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users."** NN/g, 2000-03-18. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/. Access: Full. Tier 1–2.
- Claim: N(1−(1−L)^n), L = 31%; iterative rounds of 5; distinct user groups need separate samples.
- Limitations: L is an average across unspecified projects.

**Faulkner, L. "Beyond the five-user assumption."** *Behavior Research Methods, Instruments, & Computers* 35(3), 379–383, 2003. https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03195514. Access: Abstract (via search summary). Tier 1.
- Claim: random 5-user subsets of 60 users found 55–99% of problems; worst 10-user set 80%; worst 20-user set 95%.

**Duan, P., Warner, J., Li, Y. & Hartmann, B. "Generating Automatic Feedback on UI Mockups with Large Language Models."** *CHI 2024*. https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~bjoern/papers/duan-heuristic-chi2024.pdf. Access: Partial (pp. 1–2). Tier 1.
- Claim: GPT-4 is generally accurate and helpful on poor designs; its value declines over iterations; weaker where the JSON representation differs from the visual; static single screens only.
- Limitations: I did not read the quantitative results sections.

**Guerino, G. et al. "Can GPT-4o Evaluate Usability Like Human Experts? …"** arXiv 2506.16345, 2025-06-19 (preprint). https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.16345. Access: Abstract. Tier 1 (not peer-reviewed).
- Claim: from screenshots, 21.2% overlap with the issues experts found; 27 new issues; hallucinated false positives; weak on flexibility, control and efficiency.

**W3C. WCAG 2.2 Understanding SC 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (AAA).** https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html. Access: Full. Tier 1.
- Claim: motion triggered by interaction can be disabled unless essential; rationale is vestibular disorders.

**W3C. WCAG 2.2 Understanding SC 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum) (AA).** https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html. Access: Full. Tier 1.
- Claim: 24×24 CSS px, with spacing, equivalent, inline, user-agent and essential exceptions.
- Note: the other WCAG 2.2 SC numbers in the notes (1.4.1, 1.4.3, 1.4.4, 1.4.10, 1.4.11, 2.1.1, 2.4.3, 2.4.7, 2.4.11, 4.1.2) are cited from my knowledge of the standard. I did not re-fetch them in this session.

## Rules traced

**Pernice, K. & Budiu, R. "Hamburger Menus and Hidden Navigation Hurt UX Metrics."** NN/g, 2016-06-26. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/. Access: Full. Tier 1–2.
- Evidence: 179 participants, 6 sites. Desktop hidden-navigation use 27% against 48–50%; at least 39% slower. Mobile 57% against 86%; 15% slower.
- Limitations: 2016; six sites; NN/g's own study (not peer-reviewed).

**Pernice, K. & Budiu, R. "Hamburger Menus and Hidden Navigation…" (site-level findings).** NN/g, 2016-07-24. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/find-navigation-mobile-even-hamburger/. Access: Full. Tier 1–2.
- Evidence: per-site navigation use and time to first use (for example BBC 21 s).

**Wroblewski, L. "Obvious Always Wins."** 2015-04-27. https://www.lukew.com/ff/entry.asp?1945=. Access: Full. Tier 2.
- Claim: Facebook iOS (2013) and Redbooth saw gains after moving to tab bars.
- Limitations: no numbers; the Facebook data is via TechCrunch **[QUOTED ONLY]**.

**Foster, J. (exisweb) hamburger A/B tests, 2014.** Access: Secondary (Search Engine Watch 2016, ClickZ). **[QUOTED ONLY]** Tier 4.
- Claim: about 20% more taps on "MENU" than on the icon; one site (Caffeine Informer).

**GOV.UK Design System. "Service navigation" component.** https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/service-navigation/. Access: Full (docs, as of 2026-09). Tier 1.
- Claim: collapses into a text "Menu" button on mobile by default when there is more than one item.

**Budiu, R. "Dark Mode vs. Light Mode: Which Is Better?"** NN/g, 2020-02-02. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/. Access: Full. Tier 2.
- Claim: summarises Piepenbrock 2013/2014, Dobres 2017, Aleman 2018 and Legge 1985. Light mode is better for normal vision; offer a choice.
- Limitations: Dobres, Aleman and Legge are **[QUOTED ONLY]**.

**Piepenbrock, C., Mayr, S., Mund, I. & Buchner, A. "Positive display polarity is advantageous for both younger and older adults."** *Ergonomics* 56(7), 1116–1124, 2013. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23654206/. Access: Abstract. Tier 1.

**Piepenbrock, C., Mayr, S. & Buchner, A. "Positive Display Polarity Is Particularly Advantageous for Small Character Sizes."** *Human Factors*, 2014. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0018720813515509. Access: Abstract (title and summary). Tier 1.

**While, Z. & Sarvghad, A. "Dark Mode or Light Mode? Exploring the Impact of Contrast Polarity on Visualization Performance Between Age Groups."** IEEE VIS 2024. https://arxiv.org/html/2409.10841v2. Access: Full (HTML). Tier 1.
- Evidence: 134 recruited, 52 per group analysed. No age moderation; mixed individual preference. Visualisation tasks, not reading.

**Purdue University. "Dark mode may not save your phone's battery life as much as you think…"** 2021-07-28 (Dash & Hu, MobiSys 2021). https://www.purdue.edu/newsroom/archive/releases/2021/Q3/dark-mode-may-not-save-your-phones-battery-life-as-much-as-you-think,-but-there-are-a-few-silver-linings.html. Access: Full (press release). Tier 1–2.
- Evidence: 39–47% savings at 100% brightness; 3–9% at 30–50%; four Android OLED phones.
- Limitations: press release, not the paper.

**Tversky, B., Morrison, J. B. & Bétrancourt, M. "Animation: can it facilitate?"** *IJHCS* 57, 247–262, 2002. https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf. Access: Partial (conclusion section p. 258; overall argument via fetch summary). Tier 1.

**Heer, J. & Robertson, G. "Animated Transitions in Statistical Data Graphics."** *IEEE TVCG* (InfoVis 2007). https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2007-AnimatedTransitions-InfoVis.pdf. Access: Partial (p. 1). Tier 1.
- Claim: well-designed transitions improve graphical perception; pitfalls listed. The about-1-second timing recommendation comes from the search summary.

**Nielsen, J. "How Little Do Users Read?"** NN/g, 2008-05-05 (analysis of Weinreich et al. 2008, *ACM TWEB*). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/. Access: Full. Tier 1–2.
- Evidence: 25 users, 45,237 page views; at most 28% of words read, 20% likely.
- Limitations: an unusual sample; reading inferred from dwell time. Weinreich et al. themselves are **[QUOTED ONLY]**.

**Pernice, K. "F-Shaped Pattern of Reading on the Web: Misunderstood, But Still Relevant."** NN/g, 2017-11-12. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/. Access: Full. Tier 1–2.
- Claim: the conditions for the F-pattern; alternative patterns including commitment; the F-pattern is bad.

**Laubheimer, P. "The 3-Click Rule for Navigation Is False."** NN/g, 2019-08-11. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/3-click-rule/. Access: Full. Tier 2.
- Claim: origin in Zeldman 2001 (the book itself is **[QUOTED ONLY]**); no supporting data.

**Porter, J. "Testing the Three-Click Rule."** UIE, 2003-04-16 (republished by Center Centre). https://articles.centercentre.com/three_click_rule/. Access: Full. Tier 2.
- Evidence: 44 users, 620 tasks, more than 8,000 clicks; no relation between clicks and quitting or satisfaction.

**Larson, K. & Czerwinski, M. "Web Page Design: Implications of Memory, Structure and Scent for Information Retrieval."** *CHI '98*. https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/274644.274649. Access: Abstract (via search summary). Tier 1.

**Fessenden, T. "Scrolling and Attention."** NN/g, 2018-04-15. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/. Access: Full. Tier 1–2.

**Haile, T. "What You Think You Know About the Web Is Wrong."** *Time*, 2014-03-09 (Chartbeat data). https://time.com/12933/what-you-think-you-know-about-the-web-is-wrong/. Access: Full. Tier 2.

**Tuch, A. N., Presslaber, E. E., Stöcklin, M., Opwis, K. & Bargas-Avila, J. A.
