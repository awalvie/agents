# What makes software easy to use: a one-page summary

**1. Most famous design rules are half-truths.** Rules like "three clicks maximum" lost their conditions, or never had evidence. People don't quit after three clicks; they quit when they stop feeling they're on the right track.

**2. "Intuitive" means "like something I already know".** Dropbox succeeded early partly because it was just a folder that syncs, so there was nothing new to learn. The Arc browser, loved by designers, asked people to learn many new ideas, and its makers say most people found it "too different".

**3. Hiding things has a cost.** When a website puts its main menu behind an icon, people use it far less and take longer. Hide what people rarely need, never what they use every day.

**4. Looking good and working well are different things.** Beauty shapes first impressions within a fraction of a second. It does not make a product easier to use, and a frustrating product starts to look worse. Apple's glassy iOS 26 design drew complaints that text was hard to read, and Apple added settings to tone it down.

**5. Experts need things to stay put.** People build skill on where things are. Sonos's 2024 app redesign removed features people relied on; the company estimates it cost at least $100 million in revenue, and the CEO left.

**6. Make mistakes cheap and visible.** People stop paying attention to repeated "Are you sure?" warnings, so undo protects better. The worst failures are silent. Excel quietly turns some gene names into dates, which affected about 31% of genetics papers with Excel gene lists.

**7. Defaults and friction steer people, for good or ill.** Mild tricks more than doubled sign-ups to a dubious service in an experiment, with no measurable backlash. Amazon settled with US regulators for $2.5 billion over its sign-up and cancellation flows. The same tool can protect: the bank Monzo lets customers block gambling payments, with a cooling-off period they choose themselves.

**8. Accessibility means adapting to each person.** In one study, the official standard (WCAG 2.0) covered only about half the problems blind users actually met. People's needs conflict, so the durable answer is to respect each person's own settings for text size, contrast and motion.

**9. Much "great design" is really something else.** Bloomberg's dense terminal and Craigslist's plain pages owe their market position mainly to their data and their networks of users, not their screens. Popularity and design awards are not evidence of ease of use.

**What the research could not settle**
- Whether dense professional screens beat sparse ones for experts. No controlled study exists.
- How to measure text contrast properly. The next standard's method is still undecided.
- Whether redesigns that drew backlash (Spotify, Netflix) actually hurt use. No data was published.
- How well AI reviewers work. One early study found an AI matched about a fifth of the problems human experts found.
- Products such as YouTube, Reddit and Discord were not studied, and much evidence comes from one research firm or from companies describing their own work.
