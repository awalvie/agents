Visual craft: research notes (area 3)

Researcher notes, written 2026-09-26. Sources are listed in visual-craft-sources.md. Screenshots are in visual-craft-shots/ and are dated 2026-09-26.

How to read these notes

- "(stated)" means the source states the mechanism. "(inferred)" means I derived it from the evidence. The skeptic should check every "(inferred)".
- Coverage checks. Bringhurst: I read his text only as quoted in Richard Rutter's webtypography.net, which reproduces his rules section by section. I did not read the book itself. Müller-Brockmann: I read the scanned pages 9 to 12 of Grid Systems in Graphic Design (the introduction, "Grid and design philosophy", "The typographic grid"). I did not read the worked examples. Albers: I read only the quotes on the Albers Foundation page. I did not read Interaction of Color. For all three, treat my account as the part of the book that I saw.

Finding 1. Line length: the 45 to 75 character range is a print convention. On screen, performance and preference point in different directions.

- Claim. Bringhurst's range holds for comfort and comprehension. It does not hold for speed. Longer screen lines are often read faster, and readers still prefer medium lines.
- Evidence. Bringhurst: "Anything from 45 to 75 characters is widely regarded as a satisfactory length of line for a single-column page set in a serifed text face in a text size. The 66-character line (counting both letters and spaces) is widely regarded as ideal. For multiple column work, a better average is 40 to 50 characters." Look at how narrow the scope is: a single column, a serif face, text size. "Widely regarded" is a report of consensus, not a measurement. Dyson's 2004 review of screen studies found that more characters per line often gave faster reading (for example, 100 cpl read faster than 25 cpl in Dyson & Kipping 1998). Dyson & Haselgrove (2001) found better comprehension at 55 cpl than at 100 cpl, with no speed-accuracy trade-off. Readers rated 55 cpl easiest to read, "but was not read the fastest" (Dyson 2004). In 2021 Dyson re-read the print origins (Tinker and Paterson) and found a confound: "the order of reading the texts was also not varied". She still declines to recommend long lines, because long lines "are least preferred or judged as least easy to read".
- Mechanism. A long line cuts down on scrolling and line changes (Dyson 2004 gives the reduced scrolling as a partial explanation; stated). A medium line keeps the return sweep to the next line short and gives the text a shape the reader expects (inferred; Dyson reports the preference but does not name a cause).
- Context. It applies to continuous reading of prose. It does not apply to UI labels, tables or code.
- Counterevidence. The speed findings come from small lab samples (tens of participants). The results changed with age group: in Dyson & Kipping 1997, only the 18 to 24 group read faster in the long single column. The evidence does not settle which line length is best.
- Example (observed 2026-09-26, counted from the screenshot). Wikipedia, Vector 2022 at 1920 px: body lines next to the infobox run about 90 to 99 characters. GOV.UK's passport page at 1440 px runs about 77. Both are above Bringhurst's 66 and within the range that the screen studies found fine for speed. Wikimedia's own rationale for limiting the width cites 40 to 75 cpl and a measurement of about 262 cpl at full width on 1920 px screens. Editors objected to "wasted space", and the team added a width toggle in October 2022. This shows the tradeoff between comfort for readers and density for people who scan.
- Labels. Context-dependent heuristic. B (several studies, inconsistent across them).
- When to use it. Cap the measure near 60 to 80 characters for long reading, for mixed audiences, and for older readers. Allow wider lines, or give readers a toggle, for reference content that people scan. Do not treat 66 as a target to hit exactly.

Finding 2. Screen body text works better larger than the old 16 px default. The gain stops, and line spacing matters less than designers think.

- Claim. For reading on the web, comprehension and readability improved with font size up to about 18 pt (24 px). Line spacing between 1.0 and 1.4 times the default made little difference.
- Evidence. Rello, Pielot & Marcos (CHI 2016), as summarized by Pielot: 104 participants read Wikipedia articles at six sizes (10 to 26 pt) and four line spacings (0.8, 1.0, 1.4, 1.8). Comprehension was "significantly lower for font sizes 10 and 12 points". Line spacing "had only minimal effects", with weak evidence that the two extremes hurt. Pielot converts 18 pt to 24 px. Wallace et al. (2022) cite Chatrangsan & Petrie, who found that speed and comprehension improved at 18 pt in both the UK and Thailand. GOV.UK sets body text at 19 px and keeps it at 19 px on small screens, while headings shrink (48 to 32 px for the largest heading). The design system states that the scale was "tested and iterated for readability".
- Mechanism. Larger text puts more of each word in the area of sharp vision, so each fixation takes in more (inferred; the papers report the outcome, not the optics).
- Context. It applies to text-heavy pages. It applies less to dense tools, where a larger font means fewer items on screen.
- Counterevidence. Pielot states that the recommendation "does not consider aesthetic aspects". I read only the author's summary, not the paper.
- Example. GOV.UK at 390 px (screenshot 2026-09-26): the heading drops to about two thirds of its desktop size, and the body keeps its size. The contrast in the hierarchy shrinks, and the text stays the same size to read.
- Labels. Strong heuristic. B.
- When to use it. Start reading-first directions at 18 to 20 px body. Use smaller sizes only when density is the goal and readers use the product every day.

Finding 3. A type scale is a decision-limiting device. Its ratio has no special virtue, and the right ratio changes with the size of the screen.

- Claim. A fixed set of sizes in related steps makes a hierarchy easier to read and cuts arbitrary choices. Which ratio you choose is taste and context.
- Evidence. Bringhurst: "Use the old familiar scale, or use new scales of your own devising, but limit yourself, at first, to a modest set of distinct and related intervals." He grounds this in the 16th-century scale of sizes, which "survived with little change... for 400 years" (stated: a habit with a long life, not a proof). Tim Brown (A List Apart, 2011) chose the golden ratio because it connected to his typeface's Renaissance roots, then broke his own scale for 15 px sidebar text: "Modular scales are a tool, they're not magic." James Gilyead (Utopia, 2020): "type size variation on a phone is usually relatively conservative... On a large laptop or desktop display it's often desirable to use much more dramatic type scales."
- Mechanism. Few distinct sizes mean that each step reads as a deliberate level (inferred). Related intervals make sizes that repeat recognizable as a system (Bringhurst's music analogy, stated as analogy). A small screen cannot fit the large jumps without wrapping headlines into many lines, so the ratio has to shrink (Gilyead, stated as practice).
- Counterevidence. I found no study showing that a modular ratio beats hand-tuned sizes. The claim that a ratio brings "harmony" (Brown) is an aesthetic claim. See Finding 13 on the golden ratio.
- Labels. Useful pattern. C.
- When to use it. Always limit the set of sizes. Choose the ratio for the voice you want: a tight ratio for calm, dense tools, a steep one for editorial drama. Let the ratio grow with the viewport. Break the scale when your eye says a step is wrong, and record why.

Finding 4. The choice of typeface matters less for legibility and more for voice than designers assume. What readers prefer does not predict how fast they read.

- Claim. Between common text faces at normal sizes, legibility differences are small and depend on the reader. Serifs as such make no difference. A face carries a persona that readers notice when it clashes with the content, but in one study that persona barely changed how readers perceived the text.
- Evidence. Arditi & Cho (2005, Vision Research) built a parametric font with and without serifs and found no meaningful effect on reading speed. The small advantage they found came from letter spacing. Wallace et al. (2022, TOCHI) normalized 16 fonts for x-height, width and height, and tested hundreds of crowd workers aged 18 to 71: "no single factor predicts reading speed, and interestingly, preference does not predict speed even when font size is controlled." Each participant's fastest font beat their slowest by 35%, and no font was fastest for everyone. EB Garamond had the highest average speed, but individuals read fastest in it only 48% of the time. Wallace's re-analysis of their earlier study found that font size (x-height, character height, width) affected preference, so size confounds many comparisons between fonts (stated). Brumberger (2003b): readers "had strong opinions about appropriateness" and noticed mismatches, yet "typeface persona did not have a significant impact on readers' perception of text persona". The one exception was the "serious" rating of a violent text. Her explanation is that texts with strong personas can overpower the face. Doyle & Bottomley (2006): judgments of whether a font fits a product depend partly on how the font's connotations (potency, activity) match the product's (abstract).
- Mechanism. At text size, reading depends on letter size, spacing and familiarity more than on style details (Arditi & Cho for spacing, Wallace for size, stated). A persona works at the level of expectation and first impression. The words themselves carry most of the meaning in long text (Brumberger's interpretation, stated).
- Counterevidence. Wallace's study is remote, so viewing distance was not controlled, and the authors say they "sacrifice internal validity". Brumberger used one set of faces and passages. Readers with low vision and dyslexia respond more strongly to typeface features.
- Example. The Pudding (2026-09-26) uses a heavy, tightly spaced display serif for story titles and a monospace for metadata. The persona is strong in the headlines and sits in faces that the body text does not use.
- Labels. Serif versus sans makes no difference: strong heuristic, B. Individual variation and preference against performance: B (one large study). Persona fit for display and brand: context-dependent heuristic, B/C.
- When to use it. Choose body faces for rendering at the target size, a generous x-height, open spacing and a full set of weights. Spend the persona budget on display type, where it is visible and cheap. Do not claim that a text face is "more readable" without evidence. Do not choose the text face by vote.

Finding 5. Spacing is the grouping signal, and spacing is relative. A spacing scale helps because it limits choices, not because of the number 8.

- Claim. Elements close together read as a group. A shared container groups more strongly than proximity. Space inside a group must therefore be clearly smaller than space between groups. A fixed spacing scale makes those differences consistent.
- Evidence. Proximity and similarity are core Gestalt grouping principles (Wagemans et al., 2012 review; abstract read). Palmer (1992) showed that elements in a common region group strongly, "capable of overcoming the effects of other powerful grouping factors such as proximity and similarity", and that nested regions group hierarchically (abstract). The 8-point convention: Material Design (the spec as archived in November 2014) stated "All components align to an 8dp square baseline grid. Type aligns to a 4dp baseline grid." without giving a reason on that page. Spec.fm's "8-Point Grid" (the page shows no author or date; it is commonly credited to Bryn Jackson, which I did not confirm) gives the reasons: "you automatically get a more consistent UI", "By removing 7 of every 8 spacing options, you reduce the amount of fiddling", and "Most popular screen sizes are divisible by 8 on at least one axis." It also admits that screens like 375 pt do not divide by 8 and that the irregularity is absorbed by flexible blocks.
- Mechanism. Grouping follows relative distance, so the ratio between inner and outer gaps matters more than the absolute values (inferred from the Gestalt evidence). A scale of steps makes that ratio repeat across the product (inferred). Multiples of 8 scale to whole pixels at 1.5x, 2x and 3x densities (arithmetic; Spec.fm implies it). Containers group more strongly than space, but each box, border or card adds edges, and edges add visual complexity (inferred; see Finding 12).
- Counterevidence. The "whitespace increases comprehension by almost 20% (Lin, 2004)" claim does not trace. Carl Myhill found that it began in Galitz (2007), and Lin replied that the paper "has nothing to do with whitespace". The nearest real study, Chaparro et al. (2004), found that text with margins was read more slowly but understood better, and that leading changed preference, not performance. The number 8 has no perceptual basis. Some teams use 4 as the base unit, and a denser tool may need finer steps.
- Example. Craigslist (2026-09-26) groups about a hundred links with almost no containers: tight line spacing inside each column, wider gutters between columns, and bold headings. The groups hold because the ratio between inner and outer space is large.
- Labels. Grouping by proximity and common region: fundamental principle, A. A spacing scale: useful pattern, C. The 8 in particular: design convention, D.
- When to use it. Define 5 to 8 spacing steps and assign them roles (inside a component, between siblings, between sections). Use containers when proximity alone cannot separate groups, such as mixed content or dense data. Remove them when space alone does the job.

Finding 6. A grid is a way to make a few decisions once and reuse them. It works when it is derived from the content, and it is a starting point, not a style.

- Claim. A grid reduces decisions, makes priorities visible and gives a set of pages a family resemblance. A grid derived from the canvas alone fails on the web. The strongest objection is about expression, not function.
- Evidence. Müller-Brockmann, "The typographic grid" (p. 11 of the scan): "The fewer the differences in the size of the illustrations, the quieter the impression created by the design." "Every piece of work must be studied very carefully so as to arrive at the specific grid network corresponding to its requirements." "If the text and pictures are arranged systematically, the priorities stand out more clearly." His reasons (p. 12) are "economic" (less time and cost), "rational" ("a uniform and characteristic style") and a "mental attitude" that he ties to social responsibility. The page "Grid and design philosophy" (p. 10) is openly ideological: "the will to cultivate objectivity instead of subjectivity". The line "The grid system is an aid, not a guarantee" is widely quoted from this book. I did not find it in the pages I read. Mark Boulton (2011): "create layouts from the content out", with the constraint "derived from an advertising unit, or from a thumbnail image". Nathan Ford (A List Apart, 2014) quotes Linda van Deursen: "We never start with a grid." Jan van Toorn, in the 1972 debate with Wim Crouwel (from a transcription online): grids "are highly effective for conveying a message, but that is merely a starting point." The 960 Grid System (Nathan Smith) offered 12 and 16 columns "to streamline web development workflow".
- Mechanism. A module fixes size and position relationships ahead of time, so repeated sizes become recognizable (stated by Müller-Brockmann as "quieter", "priorities stand out"). Twelve columns are popular because 12 divides by 2, 3, 4 and 6, so halves, thirds and quarters all fit (inferred, arithmetic). A content-derived grid keeps relationships true when the canvas changes size (Boulton, stated).
- Counterevidence. Van Toorn's side: a neutral system also carries values, and that neutrality can erase the designer's voice and the context. Müller-Brockmann himself treats the grid as an ethic, which is exactly what makes it a style and not a law. I found no controlled study that compares gridded and ungridded layouts directly. The nearest evidence is on symmetry and balance: Seckler, Opwis & Tuch (2015) report symmetry as the largest single factor in overall aesthetic judgments (abstract via search snippet; weak).
- Example. GOV.UK (2026-09-26) uses a two-thirds and one-third split with a wide gutter (screenshot).
- Labels. Useful pattern. C.
- When to use it. Use a strict grid for systems with many pages, editorial content and dense data. Derive the column width from the measure (Finding 1) or from the main media size. Loosen or break the grid on purpose for expressive single pages, and keep one strong alignment so that the break reads as intended.

Finding 7. Vertical rhythm and baseline grids pay off where lines sit side by side. Elsewhere they are a craft preference with a real cost.

- Claim. A consistent vertical unit makes pages calm and lines in parallel columns align. On the web a strict baseline grid is costly, and most readers of a single column never see its benefit.
- Evidence. Bringhurst (via webtypography.net 2.2.2): "Headings, subheads, block quotations, footnotes, illustrations, captions and other intrusions into the text create syncopations and variations against the base rhythm of regularly leaded lines. These variations can and should add life to the page, but the main text should also return after each variation precisely on beat and in phase." Espen Brunborg (Smashing, 2012): baseline "is notoriously difficult to get right" in CSS, and offset hacks make code "harder to maintain". He concludes that there is "no right or wrong way", while "vertical rhythm is always an important consideration". Material's 4 dp type baseline (2014) is a product version of the same idea.
- Mechanism. Aligning baselines across columns makes neighbouring lines form continuous horizontals, which the eye reads as order (inferred). In a single column, readers track one line at a time, so the benefit is small (inferred).
- Counterevidence. I found no study of reader outcomes. The reasons are aesthetic and systematic.
- Labels. Aesthetic preference, and a context-dependent heuristic for multi-column layouts. C/D.
- When to use it. Use a shared vertical unit for spacing everywhere, which is cheap. Use a true baseline grid for multi-column editorial pages and for print. Do not spend effort on it in single-column apps.

Finding 8. Emphasis is relative. Hierarchy comes from contrast against the surroundings, so de-emphasizing is often more effective than emphasizing.

- Claim. An element stands out only as far as it differs from its neighbours and those neighbours resemble each other. Adding emphasis everywhere cancels it out. Weight, colour, contrast and position build hierarchy at least as well as size does.
- Evidence. Duncan & Humphreys (1989, Psychological Review): search efficiency "increases continuously with decreasing similarity between targets and nontargets, and increasing similarity between one nontarget and another" (abstract). Apple HIG (read 2026-09-26): "Adjust font weight, size, and color as needed to emphasize important information", and "Mixing too many different typefaces can obscure your information hierarchy." Refactoring UI (Wathan & Schoger, 2018; preview chapter "Labels are a last resort"): a label:value format means "every piece of data is given equal emphasis". The fix is to "de-emphasize the label by making it smaller, reducing the contrast, using a lighter font weight", except on pages where users hunt for a label, where you do the reverse.
- Mechanism. Visual search is guided by differences in basic features (colour, size, orientation). A uniform background of distractors makes one different item pop out (Duncan & Humphreys, stated for search). Lowering the salience of secondary items raises the relative salience of the primary item without adding a new visual element (inferred for design).
- Example (2026-09-26). Stripe's hero sets one headline in two colours: the first sentence in dark ink, the rest in a lighter slate at the same size. The hierarchy comes from colour inside one typographic block, not from a second size. Linear's subtitle sits in low-contrast grey under a white headline.
- Counterevidence. Low-contrast secondary text loses legibility for some readers and in bright light. That limit is judged by the evaluation persona, so I do not repeat it here. If de-emphasis goes too far, content becomes decoration.
- Labels. Perception mechanism: fundamental principle, A. Design use: strong heuristic, C.
- When to use it. Decide the single most important item on each view first. Then lower everything else by one level before raising anything. Use size jumps for structure between sections, and weight or colour for priority within a section.

Finding 9. Colour is relative, so a palette should be built as steps of perceived lightness with assigned roles, not as a set of hex values.

- Claim. The same colour looks different in different surroundings. So a colour system is useful when its steps are equal in perceived lightness across hues and each step has a job.
- Evidence. Albers (1963): "In visual perception a color is almost never seen as it really is—as it physically is." His course begins with experience: "the beginning is not a study of color systems" (Albers Foundation). Björn Ottosson (Oklab, 2020-12-23): in HSV, colours with the same saturation and value show "clear differences in lightness for different hues", and CIELAB predicts hue badly, "in particular blue hues". Oklab aims to change lightness, chroma and hue independently. Ottosson notes that it was fitted to model data, and "this data can't be used to say which model best matches human perception". Stripe (Koopersmith & Miner, 2019) rebuilt its palette in CIELAB so that "each color... has the same contrast value at a given level", and "any two colors are guaranteed to have sufficient contrast for small text if they are at least five levels apart". Some colours turned out impossible, such as a "very colorful dark yellow". Radix Colors (docs read 2026-09-26) assigns each of 12 steps a role: 1 and 2 backgrounds, 3 to 5 component states, 6 to 8 borders, 9 and 10 solid fills, 11 and 12 text. Step 9 is the purest, highest-chroma step. Refactoring UI asks for 8 to 10 greys, because "Three or four shades... it won't be long before you wish you had something a little darker than shade #2", and warns: "You can't rely purely on math."
- Mechanism. Contrast and legibility depend on the difference in lightness. So if hues are aligned by lightness, you can swap one hue for another without breaking contrast or hierarchy (Stripe, stated). Assigning roles to steps removes the choice each time a component needs a colour (inferred from Radix). The Albers effect means that any swatch must be judged on the surface where it will sit (stated by Albers; simultaneous contrast is standard perception science).
- Counterevidence. Perceptual models are approximations (Ottosson). Yellows and some greens cannot be both dark and saturated. Designers still hand-tune (Refactoring UI).
- Labels. Relativity of colour: fundamental principle, A. Lightness-aligned role scales: strong heuristic, B/C.
- When to use it. Build every direction's palette as neutral and accent ramps in OKLCH or LCH with fixed lightness steps. Map roles to steps. Test the accent on the actual background, not on white.

Finding 10. Colour combinations. Similar hues read as harmonious, a contrasting hue makes a figure stand out, and bright colour works best in small amounts. The "60-30-10 rule" has no traceable origin.

- Claim. Harmony and emphasis pull in opposite directions. Surfaces and large areas look coherent when their hues are close. A small element pops when its hue contrasts with its ground.
- Evidence. Schloss & Palmer (2011, 48 participants, 32 colours): pair preference and harmony "both increase as hue similarity increases", with preference relying more on lightness contrast. "More preferred pairs contained cooler colors that were similar in hue and contrasting in Munsell value (lightness)". Yet "figural color preference ratings increase as hue contrast with the background increases": "Warmer figures are preferred on cooler backgrounds, cooler figures are preferred on warmer backgrounds." Eduard Imhof, as quoted in Tufte's Envisioning Information (I did not read either original): "Pure, bright or very strong colors have loud, unbearable effects when they stand unrelieved over large areas adjacent to each other, but extraordinary effects can be achieved when they are used sparingly on or between dull background tones." Reinecke & Gajos (2014, 2.4 million ratings of 430 sites from about 40,000 people): appeal peaked at a colourfulness of 6.1 on 9, but colourfulness mattered less than complexity (Cohen's d of 0.3 and 0.8 for colourfulness, against 0.6 and 2.0 for complexity).
- Mechanism. Judging a pair as a whole favours similarity, which is easy to see as one thing. Judging a figure favours contrast with its ground, which makes the figure easy to separate (Schloss & Palmer's distinction, stated). Bright colour over large areas saturates attention, so nothing stands out. In small amounts it becomes a signal (Imhof, stated as craft).
- Counterevidence. The 60-30-10 rule appears everywhere in interior-design and UI blogs with no author or study. The sources I found call its origin "unclear" or an "old wives' tale". It is a restatement of "one dominant, one supporting, a little accent" with invented precision. Reinecke found that the preferred colourfulness varied by group: women and Macedonians preferred more, and people with more education less.
- Example (2026-09-26). The Pudding breaks the Imhof rule on purpose: each story card is a large field of saturated colour (yellow, pink, orange). It works because each field frames one image and the page around it is white. Stripe keeps its saturated gradient to one diagonal band and uses one violet for all calls to action.
- Labels. Harmony versus figure contrast: context-dependent heuristic, B. Bright colours used sparingly: strong heuristic, C. 60-30-10: unsupported claim, D.
- When to use it. Choose analogous or neutral surfaces, and one contrasting accent reserved for action or meaning. Use large saturated fields only when colour is the identity and each field contains a single thing.

Finding 11. Neutrals and black. "Never use pure black" is an aesthetic preference with a narrow truth. Dark-on-light has a measured advantage for small text.

- Claim. Tinted dark greys make a palette feel cohesive. Pure black has good uses. For reading small text, dark text on a light ground performs better than the reverse.
- Evidence. Ian Storm Taylor (2012-08-07), "Design Tip: Never Use Black": real "black" objects are dark, tinted greys. Pure black "overpowers" nearby colour. Tint greys with the brand hue, more saturation the darker the grey. He admits: "I still have trouble choosing grays that set the right mood." Piepenbrock, Mayr, Mund & Buchner (2013, Ergonomics): a positive-polarity (dark-on-light) advantage for both younger and older adults. A follow-up (2014, Human Factors) found the advantage "particularly" for small characters. A third paper links it to smaller pupil size.
- Mechanism. A brighter display shrinks the pupil, which increases depth of field and sharpens the image on the retina (the Piepenbrock line of work, stated in the paper title). Tinting neutrals toward the accent hue relates them to each other, as Albers's relativity predicts (inferred).
- Counterevidence and examples (2026-09-26, pixels sampled from screenshots). Linear's page background is #08090a: almost black, slightly cool. Taylor's idea is applied, but nowhere near his #333 benchmark. Apple's iPhone hero is pure #000000 behind a product render. On black, the photographed object's edges disappear into the ground (inferred). Dark themes remain a legitimate identity choice for tools used for long hours and for media. The polarity evidence concerns reading performance, not appeal.
- Labels. "Never use black": aesthetic preference, D. Positive-polarity advantage for text: strong heuristic, B (one lab, several studies).
- When to use it. Use tinted neutral ramps by default. Use pure black for photography and product stages, and for high-contrast brands. When a direction is dark, give reading-heavy screens larger text, or a light mode.

Finding 12. Generic versus distinctive. At first glance people prefer low to moderate complexity and the look typical of the category. Novelty pays only while the design stays recognizable as its type.

- Claim. First impressions reward designs that match the category's mental model and are not cluttered. Novelty helps only when it does not reduce typicality. This is the mechanism behind "generic is sometimes right".
- Evidence. Tuch et al. (2012): company websites of high prototypicality and low complexity were rated most beautiful. Both effects appeared after 17 ms of exposure, with partial eta squared of .81 for prototypicality in study 1. "Websites of low prototypicality are generally judged as being unattractive... for websites of high as well as low complexity." Their advice: "Designs that contradict what users typically expect of a website may trigger a suboptimal first impression." Reinecke & Gajos (2014): appeal against complexity is an inverted U, peaking at 4.2 on 10. The peak moves between 2.5 and 4.8 by demographic, and "a low visual complexity has less severe consequences than high complexity does". Hekkert, Snelders & van Wieringen (2003), testing Raymond Loewy's "Most Advanced, Yet Acceptable": typicality and novelty jointly and equally predict preference but "suppress each other's effect". People "prefer novel designs as long as the novelty does not affect typicality". Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman (2004) explain such effects by processing fluency: "The more fluently perceivers can process an object, the more positive their aesthetic response." Graf & Landwehr (2015) add a second route. When people are motivated, disfluency can produce "interest" in place of displeasure. That explains why challenging designs win over engaged audiences.
- Mechanism. A typical layout is processed fluently because it matches stored expectations, and fluency feels like beauty at first sight (stated, Reber and Tuch). Clutter slows the first parse (stated). Interest needs motivation and time, which a first glance lacks (Graf & Landwehr, stated).
- Counterevidence. All three studies measure first impressions or passive viewing of screenshots. Tuch lists "passive viewing" and one site type (company sites) as limitations. A brand with nothing distinctive also gets forgotten, and none of these studies measures memorability. "Prototypical" is relative to each category: a craigslist-like page is typical for classifieds.
- Example (2026-09-26). The Pudding has a typical structure (logo, nav, card grid, date, title, dek) with atypical surfaces (sticker-style wordmark, hand-drawn icons, saturated fields). That is the MAYA split in practice. Craigslist is visually plain and dense, yet highly prototypical for its users.
- Labels. Low to moderate complexity preferred at first impression: strong heuristic, A (large, replicated). Prototypicality advantage and the MAYA interaction: strong heuristic, B.
- When to use it. For directions where trust and task come first (government, finance, checkout), keep structure and navigation typical and put identity in type, colour and imagery. For directions where attention and brand come first (a launch, editorial, a portfolio), spend novelty on one layer at a time and keep at least one layer typical. Tell the client which layer carries the risk.

Finding 13. Proportion systems (golden ratio, rule of thirds) work as constraints. Their claimed perceptual magic is weak or missing.

- Claim. Using a fixed ratio gives consistency. The claim that 1.618 or the thirds are especially beautiful is barely supported.
- Evidence. Green (1995, Perception) reviewed 130 years of golden-section experiments. He concludes that "there seems to be, in fact, real psychological effects associated with the golden section, but that they are relatively sensitive to careless methodological practices." In McManus (1980), as Green reports it, group preference for rectangles peaked broadly, spanning about 1.5:1 to 2:1, over "a heterogeneous array of individual preference patterns". Amirshahi et al. (2014) found that the rule of thirds "seems to play only a minor role" in large sets of high-quality photographs and paintings (abstract via search).
- Mechanism. Any consistent ratio creates repeated relationships that can be recognized (inferred; the same mechanism as Finding 3). Preference for moderately elongated rectangles is broad, so many ratios near 1.5 to 2 work (inferred from McManus via Green).
- Labels. Precise golden-ratio superiority: unsupported claim, D. Rule of thirds as a law: B that it is weak in excellent work. Ratios as a consistency constraint: useful pattern, C.
- When to use it. Use a ratio when it gives the team a shared rule, and choose it for the effect you want. Do not justify a direction by claiming the ratio itself makes it beautiful.

Finding 14. Icons are learned more than recognized. Familiarity beats realism, labels speed learning, and consistency comes from optical rules, not identical sizes.

- Claim. An icon's meaning comes mostly from familiarity. Text labels help until the icon is learned. A set looks consistent when shapes are sized optically on a shared grid with shared stroke and corner rules.
- Evidence. McDougall, Reppa, Kulik & Taylor (2016, Applied Ergonomics) review earlier work: familiarity "is the most important determinant of user performance and research suggests that it encompasses the effects previously attributed to concreteness", because "only a limited number of functions can be represented concretely". In their experiments, the ease of processing (fluency) "reliably influenced users' appeal ratings". Wiedenbeck (1999): novices did best with label-only and icon-plus-label buttons at first. The value of labels was "short-lived" but important for speed of learning. She recommends labelled icons whose labels can later be removed. Material Design system icons (M2 spec, read 2026-09-26): a 24 dp icon with a 20 dp live area, keyline shapes at 18 dp for a square and 20 dp for a circle, "to maintain consistent visual proportions", a 2 dp stroke, 2 dp outer corner radius and square inner corners.
- Mechanism. A circle of the same width as a square has less area and looks smaller, so the circle's keyline is larger (inferred from Material's numbers; it is the same idea as overshoot in type design, which I did not verify in a type-design source). Familiar icons are processed fluently, and fluency reads as appeal (McDougall, stated).
- Counterevidence. Familiarity favours convention, which pushes against inventive icon sets. A custom set is safe only for actions that are already familiar, or when labels are present.
- Labels. Familiarity over concreteness: strong heuristic, B. Labels speed learning: B. Optical keylines: useful pattern, C.
- When to use it. Label icons in navigation and anything new. Draw a distinctive set only on a strict shared grid, with conventional metaphors for common actions (search, close, menu, share).

Finding 15. Imagery. Images that carry information get attention. Generic decorative images are skipped.

- Claim. Photos of the actual product, place or people are looked at, often for longer than the text beside them. Generic mood photos are ignored.
- Evidence. Nielsen (NN/g, 2010), from eyetracking: "big feel-good images that are purely decorative" are "completely ignored". Photos of products and "real people" are "scrutinized". On one team page, users spent "10% more time viewing the portrait photos" than reading the bios. Stock models were ignored.
- Mechanism. Attention goes to what helps the task. An image that answers "what is it, who are they" earns fixations, and one that answers nothing gets filtered out (inferred from Nielsen's pattern).
- Counterevidence. This is one firm's eyetracking, not a controlled study. Attention is not the same as appeal: decorative imagery can still set a mood in the first-impression window (Finding 12).
- Example (2026-09-26). Apple's homepage hero is almost all product render. The Pudding's cards each show a real crop of the story's own graphic.
- Labels. Strong heuristic. C.
- When to use it. Plan imagery from the content: real product, real data, real people. Use abstract or illustrated imagery when the direction's identity depends on it, and do not expect it to carry information.

Claims that failed tracing: the 20% whitespace figure (Finding 5), 60-30-10 (Finding 10), golden-ratio beauty (Finding 13), 8 pt as optimal (Finding 5), 66 characters as ideal on screen (Finding 1).

Outside my area

- Area 4 (distinctiveness). Hekkert's MAYA and Graf & Landwehr's pleasure-interest model give a testable explanation of "generic versus distinctive". Tuch's prototypicality result argues for conventional structure in trust-heavy products.
- Area 5 (code). OKLCH ramps and Utopia's fluid type scales are the build side of Findings 9 and 3.
