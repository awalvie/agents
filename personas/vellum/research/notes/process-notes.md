# Process: research notes (area 1)

Area: how designers turn requirements into constraints, get from constraints to several directions, and choose between them. Read on 2026-09-26. Sources are in `process-sources.md`. Screenshots are in `process-shots/`.

How to read the labels. Each finding has a claim type (from the brief) and an evidence grade A–D. "Stated" means a source says it. "Inferred" means I drew it from the sources, and the source does not say it.

A caution that applies to the whole area. Most experimental work on fixation and parallel prototyping uses students, small tasks, 10–60 minute sessions, and sketches or banner ads. Vasconcelos & Crilly (2016) reviewed 25 fixation studies and found "great variety in the methods used and the results obtained", with evaluators, metrics and task complexity often unreported. Treat effect directions as more reliable than effect sizes.

Coverage check. Jansson & Smith (1991): I could not get the full text (paywalled). I used its abstract-level content as reported in three later sources that describe its method: Vasconcelos & Crilly 2016, the ICED17 fixation paper, and Crilly 2015. I did not use any number from it. Dow et al. (2010): full text read.

F1. Examples make designers copy features. Copying is not the same as lower creativity, and the effect depends on what the example is like.

- Claim. When designers see an example solution, they tend to reuse its key features, including its flaws. How much this harms the result depends on the example's distance from the problem, its fidelity, novelty, diversity and timing. It also depends on the task instructions.
- Evidence.
  - Jansson & Smith (1991), as reported in Vasconcelos & Crilly (2016) and Crilly (2015). Students, and in one experiment professional engineers, saw a picture of a flawed solution. They repeated its features even when told not to. Stated.
  - Marsh, Landau & Hicks (1996), abstract. Conformity to examples appeared in all three experiments. But "examination of total output, elaborateness of design, and the noncritical features did not confirm that the conformity effect constrained creative output". More examples increased conformity. A delay before the task increased conformity. Stated.
  - Purcell & Gero (1996), as reported in Vasconcelos & Crilly. They found "little evidence of fixation" in general. Mechanical engineers fixated more than industrial designers. Fixation may attach to the principle of the example rather than its surface features. Stated by the review.
  - Sio, Kotovsky & Cagan (2015), a meta-analysis of 43 studies; I read the abstract only, as reported in a search summary and in Vasconcelos & Crilly. Examples reduced the variety of ideas and narrowed exploration, but improved the novelty and quality of ideas. A single uncommon example helped most. Stated.
  - Vasconcelos & Crilly (2016), a review, full text. The effects vary with the example:
    - Fidelity: partial photographs and verbal clues gave more original ideas than full photographs or full solution descriptions (Cheng et al. 2014; Gonçalves et al. 2012).
    - Distance: stimuli "neither too near nor too far" from the problem did best.
    - Diversity: diverse stimuli gave more categories but shallower exploration of each.
    - Timing: examples given on request, when a designer knows they are stuck, gave more novel ideas than examples pushed at them (Siangliulue et al. 2015).
    - Stated by the review.
  - Kulkarni, Dow & Klemmer (2012, CogSci; N=81). Examples increased conformity at every timing. Early and repeated exposure improved creativity ratings. Late exposure increased conformity without improving creativity. Stated.
- Counterevidence and disagreement.
  - On instructions, the studies conflict. Jansson & Smith, and Smith et al. (1993), found that telling people to avoid the example's features did not stop copying. Chrysikou & Weisberg (2005; N=89 and N=60) found that "defixating instructions" reduced it. The difference may depend on whether the instruction names the specific bad features. That is inferred. The review calls the evidence "equivocal".
  - Practitioners disagree on whether to look at the market first:
    - One consultant in Crilly (2015): researching existing products first is "polluting your mind".
    - Designers in Herring et al. (2009; N=11) routinely start with a "competitive audit". They use it to find what is already taken and to explain why their design is unique.
    - Designers in Lucero (2012; N=14) avoid competitor images and same-domain images on mood boards. In their words, "they are already known".
    - The resolution I infer: near examples serve differentiation and feasibility checks, and far or abstract material serves generation. Keep the two uses apart.
- Context. Early concept generation. Short tasks. Mostly novices.
- Example. Lucero's designers put on mood boards images from outside the domain, so that the board sets a direction without supplying a template.
- Labels. Copying of example features: fundamental principle, A (replicated many times). That copying lowers creativity: context-dependent heuristic, B/C (conflicting results). The moderators (distance, fidelity, timing): context-dependent heuristic, C.
- When to use.
  - For a persona that looks at references: use far-domain, partial or abstracted references to generate ideas. Use near competitors only to check what is taken or conventional. Look at references early, or when you are stuck, not as a template to fill.
  - When a reference is flawed or on-brand for someone else, name the specific features not to reuse (Chrysikou & Weisberg).
  - Do not use this finding to ban references. Examples also raised quality in the meta-analysis.

F2. Fixation has a useful twin: persistence. Experts commit early to one concept and defend it, and this is partly what makes them effective.

- Claim. The same behaviour counts as fixation or as persistence. New concepts always have problems. Dropping a concept at the first difficulty and trading it for an unbuilt alternative is its own failure.
- Evidence.
  - Crilly (2015). Interviews with 13 consultancy designers, average 21 years of experience; full text read. One participant: "sometimes the worst thing you can do is to ditch what you have got and jump ship for some other concept that looks more appealing, it only looks more appealing because it hasn't been built yet!" Crilly frames the core tension as openness against persistence. Stated.
  - Cross (2004), a review, full text:
    - Rowe (1987): "considerable effort is made to make the initial idea work, rather than to stand back and adopt a fresh point of departure."
    - Ullman et al. (1988): designers "typically pursue only a single design proposal".
    - Darke (1979): architects work from "primary generators".
    - Cross concludes that generating a wide range of alternatives "is recommended by theorists and educationists but appears not to be normal practice for expert designers". He adds: "Generating a very wide range of alternatives may not be a good thing."
    - All stated.
  - Fricke (1996), via Cross. Engineering designers who generated very few concepts fixated. Those who generated very many spent their time managing variants instead of evaluating them. The successful ones ran a "balanced search". Stated, in the secondary source.
  - Ball, Evans & Dennis (1994), abstract. Semi-expert engineers followed a top-down, depth-first procedure, with 12% deviation. They "focused exclusively on initial, satisfactory (but usually sub-optimal) solution concepts rather than comparing alternatives". Stated.
  - Nijstad, De Dreu, Rietzschel & Baas (2010), abstract. Originality comes by two paths: flexibility, meaning many categories, or persistence, meaning depth in a few categories. Stated. This gives a mechanism for why both breadth and commitment can work.
- Counterevidence.
  - Kim & Ryu (2014), via Vasconcelos & Crilly: experts are more committed to their own concepts, and so may fixate more than novices.
  - Crilly's designers said experience of failure makes designers more conservative.
  - Bilalić et al. (2009), abstract: chess experts search more in depth inside their specialty and more in breadth outside it. Expert depth-first work is therefore domain-bound.
- Context. Experts in familiar problem types (Lloyd & Scott, via Cross). The advantage probably shrinks on unfamiliar problems. That is inferred from Bilalić.
- Labels. Context-dependent heuristic, B (several protocol and interview studies agree; small samples).
- When to use.
  - Once a direction is chosen, the persona should push through its first problems before abandoning it.
  - At the divergence stage, the persona should not accept a single early concept. It should produce a small number of genuinely different frames: a balanced search, not 30 variants.
  - The number of directions is not set by any source. Fricke gives no number.

F3. Making several designs before getting feedback beats making them one at a time. The benefit needs the alternatives to be compared, and shown.

- Claim. Parallel prototyping, meaning several alternatives made before feedback, gives better and more diverse results than serial prototyping, meaning feedback after each one. The mechanisms are comparison, delayed evaluation, and emotional distance from any single design.
- Evidence.
  - Dow et al. (2010), full text. 33 novices made web banner ads in 10-minute prototype periods. Parallel: 3 ads, feedback, 2 ads, feedback, final. Serial: feedback after each of 5.
    - Parallel ads had higher click-through: 445.0 vs 397.9 clicks per million impressions, p<0.05.
    - Parallel ads had higher expert ratings: 24.4 vs 21.7 out of 50.
    - Parallel ads were rated more diverse.
    - Parallel participants gained self-efficacy. 8 of 17 serial participants described the critique as negative, against 0 parallel participants.
    - The authors' reading: "With only one option there is no separation between designer and design."
    - Stated.
  - Dow et al. (2011), CHI; 84 participants in pairs. "Share Multiple" (make 3, show 3) beat "Share Best" (make 3, show the best one) and "Share One" (make 1, show 1) on ratings. Share Best did not differ from Share One (Tukey, p>0.05). Share Multiple pairs borrowed more of each other's features and took more conversational turns, and group rapport rose. Stated.
  - Tohidi et al. (2006), CHI; 48 participants. Users rated a design higher, and criticised it less, when they saw it alone than when they saw it among three alternatives. Stated.
- The mechanism I draw from Dow 2011 (inferred). Making alternatives privately and then presenting only the winner loses most of the gain. The gain comes from the alternatives being visible side by side during critique.
- Limits and counterevidence.
  - Small N. Short timed tasks. Novices. One genre (ads).
  - The similarity analyses treat thousands of pairwise judgements as independent (F(1,14816)). This likely overstates significance. That is my inference, not the authors'.
  - Dow 2010: experienced participants made less diverse ads that were rated better. Experts prune.
  - Dow 2011 found click-through and ratings did not correlate. "Better" depends on the measure.
  - The authors themselves call for longer timescales and teams.
- Labels. Strong heuristic, B (a consistent small set of lab studies from one group, plus Tohidi).
- When to use.
  - Always, in the persona's case, because its job is several directions. Present them side by side, with the comparison made explicit, not as one recommendation with hidden runners-up.
  - Less useful when the problem is well solved and a convention is clearly right (see F12).

F4. Iteration refines. It does not diverge. Divergence has to be built into the process.

- Claim. Repeated build-test cycles improve a concept, but designers who iterate keep to their first idea. Exposure to others' ideas narrows the range of ideas.
- Evidence.
  - Dow, Heddleston & Klemmer (2009); N=28; egg-drop task. The iteration condition beat the no-iteration condition. But "iteration did not necessarily lead to more divergence". One participant: "This is what I thought of first". Stated.
  - Dow et al. (2010). Later serial designs were rated more similar to each other. The serial participant said: "I pretty much stuck with the same idea." Stated.
  - Kohn & Smith (2010), abstract. Exchanging ideas in brainstorming "reduced the number of domains of ideas", and ideas conformed to others' suggestions. Stated.
  - Girotra, Terwiesch & Ulrich (2010), abstract. Building on others' ideas was "counterproductive". Build-up ideas were neither more numerous nor better. Stated.
- Counterevidence. Dow 2011 found that borrowing features across shared multiple designs went with better results. Cross-pollination between finished alternatives may help. Cross-pollination during generation narrows. That reconciliation is inferred.
- Labels. Strong heuristic, B.
- When to use.
  - The persona should not produce its directions as successive revisions of one draft, which is serial and fixating. It should generate the frames independently first, then develop each.
  - Borrow between directions only after each exists.

F5. Individuals working alone, then together, generate more and better ideas than groups brainstorming aloud. Group sessions still serve other purposes.

- Claim. Nominal groups (people alone, ideas pooled) outperform interactive brainstorming groups on the quantity and quality of ideas. The main cause is production blocking: only one person can speak at a time.
- Evidence.
  - Diehl & Stroebe (1987), abstract: production blocking "accounted for most of the productivity loss". Stated.
  - Mullen, Johnson & Salas (1991), a meta-analysis, abstract: brainstorming groups were less productive "in terms of both quantity and quality". The loss was larger in larger groups. Stated.
  - Girotra et al. (2010): the hybrid structure (individuals first, then the group) generated more ideas and better ideas, and judged their quality better. Stated.
  - Practice. Knapp (2016, GV design sprint) reviewed the outcomes of his Google brainstorm workshops and wrote: "Not a single new idea generated in the brainstorms had been built or launched." Sprint sketches are made alone, shown unsigned, and critiqued before the author speaks. Stated. This is one practitioner's self-report.
- Counterevidence. Sutton & Hargadon (1996), a year of fieldwork at IDEO. Brainstorms served organisational memory, skill variety, a "status auction", impressing clients, and income. Efficiency of idea generation was not the point. Stated in the abstract.
- Labels. Nominal beats interactive for idea output: fundamental principle, A. The hybrid structure: strong heuristic, B.
- When to use. Mainly for teams. For the persona, the analogue is to generate each direction in isolation before comparing, so that one direction does not anchor the rest. That is inferred.

F6. Choosing is the weak step. People reject the novel option, overrate their own, and evaluate worse after switching into judging mode.

- Claim. At selection time, evaluators prefer feasible and familiar ideas over original ones, even when originality is the stated goal. Creators overrate their own ideas. Generating ideas right before evaluating others' ideas improves forecasts, and switching into pure evaluation removes that gain.
- Evidence.
  - Rietzschel, Nijstad & Stroebe (2010), abstract. Participants selected "feasible and desirable ideas, at the cost of originality". Telling them to select original ideas worked, but lowered their satisfaction and the rated effectiveness of the chosen ideas. Stated.
  - Mueller, Melwani & Goncalo (2012), abstract. Under uncertainty, people show a bias against creativity relative to practicality, and it "interfered with participants' ability to recognize a creative idea". Stated.
  - Berg (2016), 339 circus professionals and forecasts checked against 13,248 audience members, plus a lab experiment:
    - Creators forecast others' novel ideas better than managers did, but not their own. They overestimated their own.
    - Managers committed more false negatives on novel ideas.
    - In the lab, generating ideas before ranking improved accuracy. An evaluation task done after generating erased the gain.
    - Stated.
  - Case: Mozilla and johnson banks (2016–17 open rebrand), Murray & Johnson retrospective. The brand group scored the first routes against five criteria. "'Protocol' in its original form received some of the lowest scores." Protocol became the final identity. Stated. This is one case, told by the team, and shows intent, not an outcome measure.
- Counterevidence. The feasibility preference is not always wrong. Crilly's designers chose "acceptable" over "superior" when the client's money and time were short, and knew they were doing it. Rietzschel's instruction to pick original ideas cost perceived effectiveness.
- Labels. Strong heuristic, B (several independent studies, different methods).
- When to use.
  - When the persona recommends one of its own directions, assume it overrates its favourite and underrates the strangest one. Make the criteria explicit.
  - Keep at least one novel direction alive into the comparison, and say plainly what it risks.
  - Let the requester choose. The persona should not silently filter out the risky option before showing the set (compare F3, Dow 2011 Share Best).

F7. Constraints help creativity up to a point. Which constraint, and how abstractly the brief is stated, change what comes out.

- Claim. Constraints on inputs, process and outputs tend to raise creativity at moderate levels and lower it at extremes. Removing the obvious option forces a search away from the "path of least resistance". A brief that is too abstract gives off-target ideas. A brief that is too concrete, or that lacks context, gives self-similar ones.
- Evidence.
  - Acar, Tarakci & van Knippenberg (2019), a review of 145 studies across management fields; partial full text. They propose an inverted U. Their own caution: for output constraints, "some qualitative evidence indicates an inverted U-shape relationship" (Onarheim 2012; Rosso 2014). The curve is a synthesis proposal, not a measured curve. Stated.
  - Moreau & Dahl (2005), abstract: "input constraints encourage more creative processing, provided the individual is not under significant time constraints". Stated.
  - Zahner et al. (2010) and Liikkanen & Perttula (2008), via Vasconcelos & Crilly:
    - Abstract problem statements increased the number, domains and originality of ideas.
    - Concrete statements gave solutions that fit the constraints better.
    - A missing context can trigger "a contextual defaulting strategy, therefore leading to self-similar solutions".
    - Stated by the review.
  - Practice. Basecamp, *Shape Up* (Singer 2019), chapter 2 (screenshot `basecamp-shapeup-dotgrid-2026-09-26.png`):
    - The "appetite" (the time a problem is worth, not an estimate) turned "add a calendar" into "which tenth?", which became a read-only two-month dot grid.
    - "Wireframes are too concrete. Words are too abstract."
    - Stated. This is the team's own account.
- Mechanism (inferred from the above). A constraint helps when it removes the default answer while leaving a space to search. It hurts when it removes the space. Time pressure removes the search itself (Moreau & Dahl).
- Labels. Constraints can help: strong heuristic, B. The inverted U: context-dependent heuristic, C (proposed, not measured in design). Brief abstraction: context-dependent heuristic, C.
- When to use.
  - The persona should turn requirements into a few binding constraints: audience, the one job, content, platform, appetite for complexity. It should state the defaults it will refuse for each direction.
  - When a brief lacks context, ask for it or state assumptions. Otherwise all directions default to the same generic answer (Liikkanen & Perttula).
  - Different directions can take different self-imposed constraints, for example "no imagery" or "type only". This is a way to force real difference. Inferred.

F8. Designers do not fix the problem first. They frame it through early solution conjectures, and the most obvious "insight" is often the one everybody has.

- Claim. Problem and solution co-evolve. Designers reach a frame, a problem-solution pairing, through early conjectures, and they invent requirements while sketching. An insight that feels original can be the predictable product of salient information in the brief.
- Evidence.
  - Dorst & Cross (2001), protocol study of 9 experienced industrial designers, full text. All nine arrived at the same "keep newspapers separate" idea, and all reported it as original. So had all 21 students in earlier studies with the same brief. The authors: "certain kinds of information in the problem data may spur similar 'creative' concepts". Designers checked the brief against a "default project" and looked for "surprises". Stated.
  - Suwa, Gero & Purcell (2000), abstract. In one architect's session, "unexpected discoveries" in sketches and the invention of requirements drove each other in about half of the process. Stated. N=1.
  - Cross (2004). Designers "move rapidly to early solution conjectures". Framing "is seldom done in one burst at the beginning" (Schön). Problem structuring recurred throughout (Goel & Pirolli 1992). Stated.
  - Paton & Dorst (2011), abstract. 15 visual communication designers elicit the client's frame during briefing, reframe it, and reflect it back. Stated.
  - Hay et al. (2017), a systematic review of 47 protocol studies. Findings are fragmented, with "considerable conceptual and terminological differences" between studies. Stated. This limits the strength of everything in F8.
- Counterevidence. Ball et al. (1994) found engineers mostly structured and top-down. The co-evolution picture fits open-ended product and architecture briefs better than well-specified engineering ones. That is inferred.
- Labels. Co-evolution: strong heuristic, B (many protocol studies, small N). "Obvious insight is shared": useful pattern, C (one strong case).
- When to use.
  - For the persona, directions should differ in their frame, meaning what the design is fundamentally about, not only in styling.
  - Treat the first "clever" idea the brief suggests as the default direction that others will also reach. Keep it if it is right (see F12), but label it as the expected one and make the other directions depart from it.

F9. Fidelity sets the kind of feedback you get. Rough invites questions about direction. Polished invites comments on details. Practitioners disagree on which to show.

- Claim. The finish of a representation signals how committed it is and steers critique. A sketch "suggests" and a prototype "describes" (Buxton). Polished work draws detail-level feedback and judgement of the surface. Rough work leaves room but can be dismissed as unfinished.
- Evidence.
  - Buxton (2007), his own excerpt. Sketches have an "appropriate degree of refinement", meaning they should "not suggest a degree of refinement of the concept depicted that exceeds the actual state of development". They are "plentiful", with meaning "in the context of a collection". Stated. This is craft opinion from an expert, grade C.
  - *Shape Up*. "Work that's too fine, too early commits everyone to the wrong details." The authors pitch shaped work as fat-marker sketches and "breadboards". Stated.
  - Lucero (2012). Mood-board designers choose the level of abstraction per client: "If you go too concrete … the attention is caught by" specifics. Concrete discussion "tends to narrow the number of directions". Stated.
  - Mozilla retrospective (screenshot `mozilla-roads-not-taken-2026-09-26.png`): "no matter how many times we said that these were early explorations we received feedback that they were too 'unpolished.' Meanwhile, others felt that they were too polished". Viewers also judged whole identity systems "by its logo alone". Stated.
  - Counter-position. Apple, as reported by Walters (BusinessWeek, 2008) from Michael Lopp's SXSW talk: "pixel perfect mockups" remove ambiguity. Stated second-hand, grade C.
- The disagreement depends on (inferred): who is judging and what decision is open. Pixel-perfect work suits a team that has already chosen a direction and is settling the details. Rough work suits choosing between directions, and viewers who can read sketches. Lucero's designers note that people without visual training struggle with abstract boards.
- Labels. Context-dependent heuristic, C.
- When to use. The persona's outputs are working HTML files, which are high fidelity by nature, so it cannot use roughness. Inferred mitigations:
  - Make the directions differ at the level of the frame, so that the comparison cannot collapse into surface preference.
  - Say in the explanation which decisions each file commits to and which are placeholders.
  - Avoid finishing the details unevenly across directions. The most polished one will win on polish.

F10. How many directions, and how different. What matters is the best one, so spread matters more than the average. Machines and teams both drift toward the same idea.

- Claim. When the goal is the best idea, not the average idea, the quality of the result depends on the number of ideas, their average quality, their variance, and the ability to recognise the best one. Directions that share one core principle give little extra value.
- Evidence.
  - Girotra et al. (2010), abstract: the quality of the best idea depends on these four variables. Stated.
  - Crilly (2015), a consultant: "I'm going to come up with ten different concepts … five of them share the same core principle!" Stated.
  - Apple, as reported by Walters (2008) from Lopp: 10 unrestricted mockups, narrowed to 3, then to 1. Stated second-hand. Grade C, and a claim about intent.
  - Meincke, Mollick & Terwiesch (2024), preprint, GPT-4, 35 prompting strategies, idea diversity measured by cosine similarity:
    - Base GPT-4 pools were less diverse than a pool from humans working separately (0.243 for humans vs 0.255–0.432 across strategies).
    - Chain-of-thought prompting nearly closed the gap. It works by first listing many short ideas, then making them "bold and different", then elaborating.
    - Pools from different strategies overlapped little.
    - Persona prompts ("Steve Jobs") were weaker.
    - Stated.
  - Doshi & Hauser (2024), abstract. Access to LLM ideas made individual stories rated more creative, but the stories were "more similar to each other". Stated.
  - Wadinambiarachchi et al. (2024), CHI abstract, N=60. An AI image generator during ideation raised fixation on the initial example and lowered the number, variety and originality of ideas. Stated.
- Unsupported, traced. "Quantity leads to quality", told as the *Art & Fear* ceramics class, is a single anecdote. Its co-author told James Clear it was a photography class run by Jerry Uelsmann, retold with ceramics "to broaden the range of media". Kleon (2020) quotes the email. There is no data behind it. Label: unsupported claim, D.
- Labels. Variance matters: strong heuristic, B. LLM homogenisation: strong heuristic, B (independent studies, different media).
- When to use.
  - The persona should decide first on the axes along which the directions differ, such as frame, tone, density or convention. Then check that no two directions share a core principle.
  - The list-first, then diversify, then elaborate sequence is the only method here with measured effect on LLM idea diversity.
  - No source gives a correct number of directions. Apple's "10 to 3" is a report of practice, not a finding.

F11. Show alternatives, not a single answer. Showing one polished direction or a merged compromise has costs of its own.

- Claim. Presenting several real alternatives gets more candid critique and helps stakeholders understand the space. Clients fixate on the first concept they love. Merging directions by addition yields a compromise. Practitioners disagree on whether to present options at all.
- Evidence.
  - For alternatives: Tohidi et al. (2006) and Dow et al. (2011), see F3. Crilly (2015): clients "want to see that we've explored a lot of areas", and that expectation itself pushed designers wider. A charismatic first sketch can make a client "blindly" commit: "it's difficult to get them distracted from [that first idea]". Stated.
  - Against: Paul Rand to Steve Jobs on the NeXT logo (Jobs, 1993 interview with Doug Evans and Alan Pottasch, quoted by several secondary sources): "I will solve your problem for you and you will pay me. You don't have to use the solution. If you want options go talk to other people." Rand's presentation book instead walked the client through the reasoning. Stated. This is one famous case and shows a stance, not a result.
  - Fake options. Lopp, via Walters: Apple's ten are not "seven in order to make three look good". The "duck" story, an Interplay lore anecdote reported by Atwood (2012): an artist added a decoy duck for a producer to remove. Anecdote, D. Both describe decoy options, which spend the client's attention on nothing.
  - Merging:
    - Frey et al. (2009), simulation models: Pugh's controlled convergence works by rating concepts against a strong datum, then using the matrix to prompt new ideas and hybrids. It beat a single summary score and a Borda count across a "substantial range of assumptions". Stated. A critique by Hazelrigg (2010) exists; I did not read it.
    - Mozilla retrospective: "Simply putting the two routes together … looked exactly like what it is: two routes mashed together. A boardroom compromise to please no-one." The final identity came from rethinking Protocol with the energy of the other routes, not from pasting them together. Stated, one case.
- The disagreement depends on (inferred): trust and authority. Rand could refuse options because the client had hired his judgement. Where the client is choosing the direction, as for this persona, alternatives work better. Hybrids need a new organising idea. Otherwise they average out what made each direction distinct.
- Labels. Showing alternatives: strong heuristic, B. Merging needs re-framing: useful pattern, C.
- When to use. The persona presents its directions as real alternatives, each able to win, with no decoys. If asked to merge, it should find one idea that carries the chosen strengths, not stack features, and say what distinctiveness the merge costs.

F12. The conventional direction often wins. Generic is a legitimate option when the job is explanation or familiarity.

- Claim. In comparison, a plain conventional solution frequently beats a clever one, especially for tasks where the user must understand quickly. Experts' less diverse work was rated better.
- Evidence.
  - Knapp (2016), a Slack onboarding sprint. Of ten sketches, a "clever and ambitious" one and a "traditional approach" that "spent a lot of time thinking through the text and steps" reached testing. "The boring, traditional approach was the clear winner — it actually explained the product." Stated, one case, a practitioner account.
  - Dow et al. (2010): experienced participants' ads were less diverse and better rated. The authors: "experts may know a priori which areas are promising". Stated.
  - Crilly (2015): under budget and time limits, an "acceptable solution" found quickly is often rationally preferred. Stated.
- Counterevidence. Rietzschel (F6) shows that people prefer the conventional option even when originality was the goal. So "the conventional one won" needs a check: did it win on the task, or through selection bias? Mozilla: the favourites "hogged the spotlight" and were later dropped.
- Labels. Context-dependent heuristic, C.
- When to use.
  - The persona should usually include one direction that is deliberately conventional and done very well. It is a strong candidate when the product must be learned fast or matches a well-known category.
  - Label it as the conventional baseline, which is also Pugh's "datum", so the other directions are judged against it.

F13. Critique helps only if it is aimed at the work, separated from authority, and asked for specifically.

- Claim. Feedback often harms performance, especially when it draws attention to the self. Critique works better when the critics cannot impose changes, when the presenter says what feedback is wanted, and when the work is anonymous or one of several.
- Evidence.
  - Kluger & DeNisi (1996), a meta-analysis of 607 effect sizes, abstract. The average effect was positive (d=.41), but "over 1/3 of the FIs decreased performance". Effectiveness "decreases as attention moves up the hierarchy closer to the self and away from the task". Stated. Grade A for the general claim, not specific to design.
  - Pixar. Catmull (First Round Review interview, 2015): "the Braintrust should have no authority". Colleagues could be "extremely candid without anyone feeling attacked". The book version, *Creativity, Inc.* (2014), says: "the director does not have to follow any of the specific suggestions given". I have the book quote only through secondary sources. Stated, a team's account of intent.
  - Figma (Levin 2019). Critique is explicitly not the forum for "making major product decisions". The presenter shows "Here's the feedback I am looking for" and "Here's what I'm NOT looking for". Round-the-room turns counter the "loudest voice". The team's own list of problems before the change: "group think", "+1s", "too 'nice' rather than 'honest'". Stated.
  - GV sprint (Knapp 2016): sketches are unsigned, and "the creator remains silent" until others have spoken. Stated.
  - Dow et al. (2010): a follow-up (N=30) found targeted critique beat random critique and no critique (ratings 15.9 vs 15.1 vs 14.4 out of 30). Stated. The effect is small.
  - Pile-on. Mozilla: "As soon as one person remarked 'It looks like a stapler,' the entire audience of blog readers piled on". The Dino route "died a slow and painful death". Stated.
- Counterevidence. Public, unstructured feedback in the Mozilla case also did useful work, "an impromptu IP check with the world". Tohidi found users give critique, not solutions: usability testing "is a means to identify problems, not provide solutions".
- Labels. Feedback can harm, and self-focus harms most: fundamental principle, A. Critique separated from authority: useful pattern, C.
- When to use.
  - When the persona asks for or responds to critique, it names the decision the feedback should inform.
  - It treats comments as problems to diagnose, not as instructions.
  - It does not let one vivid comment kill a direction without checking it against the brief.

F14. Process diagrams such as the Double Diamond communicate. They do not describe how experts work.

- Claim. Stage models (diverge, converge, diverge, converge) are useful for explaining work to clients. Observed design is opportunistic, and it alternates between breadth and depth.
- Evidence.
  - Eisermann (2023?, Design Council Medium; the post has no visible date in the archived copy, so the date is uncertain). He co-created the Double Diamond. Its objective was "to help their clients understand what designers do". It is "linear" and has no feedback loop for measurement. Is it still fit for purpose: "probably not". Stated.
  - Guindon (1990), via Cross 2004, three software designers: they "frequently deviate from a top-down approach". Visser (1990): a plan used "in an opportunistic way". Ball & Ormerod, via Cross: experts "alternate between breadth-first and depth-first modes". Stated in the secondary source.
  - Ball, Onarheim & Christensen (2010), abstract via search summary, software designers: a breadth-first first pass on easy requirements, then depth-first on complex ones, where epistemic uncertainty rose. Stated.
- Labels. The diagram is a communication device: design convention, C. Experts alternate breadth and depth: strong heuristic, B.
- When to use.
  - The persona can use diverge/converge language to explain itself.
  - It should not follow the stages rigidly. A sensible order, inferred from Ball et al. 2010: a breadth-first pass across all directions on the easy requirements, then depth on the hardest requirement within each direction, because that is where directions really differ or break.

What I could not settle.

- The right number of directions. No source measured it for interface design. Fricke's "balanced search" gives no number, and Apple's "10→3→1" is second-hand.
- Whether designing directly in high-fidelity HTML changes fixation. I found no study. The prototyping evidence is mixed: Youmans (2011) found building reduced fixation; Viswanathan & Linsey found sunk-cost attachment (both via Vasconcelos & Crilly). The sunk-cost effect is plausible for code, which costs effort to change. That is inferred.
- Lawson's constraint model (*How Designers Think*). I could not access the text, so I did not use it.
- Critique research on crowd feedback (Krause et al. 2017, Yuan et al. 2016). I could not reach the texts and did not use them.

Outside my area.

- Evaluation (the judging persona). Tohidi et al. (2006): users inflate ratings of a design seen alone. Comparative testing reduces the inflation. Usability testing surfaces problems, not solutions.
- Area 4 (generic look). Doshi & Hauser (2024) and Meincke et al. (2024) measure how LLM output homogenises, and the second shows prompting that widens it. Dorst & Cross (2001) show that a brief's salient information yields the same "original" idea in every designer. This may help explain generic-looking AI designs.
- Area 3 or 4. In the Mozilla retrospective, palette decisions were constrained by UI use: the neon palette was "breathtaking" but impractical for web properties, and the softer colours came from the browser highlight bar. Typeface choice came from redrawing needs. This is an example of brand expression meeting interface constraints.
