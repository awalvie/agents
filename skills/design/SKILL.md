# Design

Design for the people who use the thing first, then its form. Every design choice is yours.
Ask the person only for a fact that you cannot find, or for a verdict on a finished round. Do
not present a design that did not pass [checklist.md](checklist.md).

If the thing is a screen of an app or a web page, also follow [apps.md](apps.md).

Words in this skill:

- The thing: what you design, for example a screen, a page, a tool or a document.
- A prototype: the thing in a form that people can judge, for example a screenshot of the real
  app, a sample run of a tool, or a draft.
- A round: the prototypes that you present at one time.

## 1. Understand

1. Load this skill and [checklist.md](checklist.md) at the start of each new thing, even if you
   used them on the last one.
2. Read the decisions so far and the project's rules. Do not ask a decided question again.
3. Look at the thing as it is now, in each of its states. Record each state.
4. Look at how two or three other products solve the same jobs.
5. Write the brief: your answers to part 1 of the checklist, each part with its job, what is
   missing, and what the other products do. Then sketch the structure.
6. Give the brief, the sketch and part 1 of the checklist to a second agent. Ask what is wrong
   or missing. Make no prototype until it passes.

## 2. Make versions

- For a change to one part, change that part only. For a whole thing, make one round for the
  structure, then rounds for the details.
- Make two to four versions that differ in approach, not in detail. Recommend one.
- If the thing has more than one form, for example a phone and a laptop, design each form. Do
  not stretch one into the other.
- Do not present a version again after the person rejected it.

## 3. Make prototypes

- Make each prototype as close to the real thing as you can. For software, apply the version
  to the real product with local data.
- Make one for each state, with content in three amounts: none, little and uneven, and a lot.
  Ask what the real content looks like, and match it.
- Before you change the person's files, save their state. After, undo only your changes, and
  make sure that their files are as they were. Never use a command that discards their work.

## 4. Check, then review

1. Look at each prototype as a whole, then part by part, then at the details, after the last
   change. Each closer look finds faults that the wider one hides.
2. Write your answers to parts 2 to 5 of the checklist on the review page. Fix each "no", and
   make the prototypes again.
3. Look at each prototype as the person. Read their past corrections, then name what they would
   reject and why. Look hardest at what you added since the last round.
4. Give a reviewer, or a second agent, the prototypes, the checklist, the person's decisions,
   their past corrections and their last complaint. Ask it to answer parts 2 to 5 itself, and
   what the person would reject. Do not give it your answers, and do not tell it that a part is
   settled. Where its answer differs from yours, the item is "no".
5. Apply the correct findings. If a finding reverses a decision, ask the person. Reject the rest
   with a reason.
6. Stop after two review cycles. List the findings that are still open.

## 5. Present

- Start the review page with the open question. Below it, put the thing as it is and the
  prototype side by side, in each state, with the changes marked.
- Keep one short list of decisions, on the review page and in the decisions file.
- In the reply, name the reviewer and what its review changed.

## After the answer

- If the person accepts the design, build it. Then start the next thing.
- If the person rejects a design, go back to part 1. Find the job or the user fact that you got
  wrong.
- If the person rejects an approach two times, ask what they want instead.
- Turn each lesson into a checklist item or a project rule.
